# 06 — Geographic Expansion Beyond Chicago

**Prepared:** 2026-11-04 · **Phase 6 of 7** · Research only. Labels: **[V]** primary/official · **[S]** secondary · **[A]** assumption · **[I]** inference · **[M]** background knowledge (pre-2026 Census-scale figures; verify before use).
**Scoring model:** reproducible. The weights and sub-scores below are the whole model; change them and re-sum.

## Bottom line

1. **The next move is not a new city. It is the Chicago suburbs plus the NW Indiana border.** This scores highest under every weighting tested. Illinois taxes pouches at 45% of wholesale (~$1.80 on a $4-wholesale can); **Indiana taxes them at $0.50/oz (~$0.13 per can)**, the second-lowest of any taxing state [V/S: Tax Foundation, Sept 15, 2026]. That ~$1.65/can tax gap, 25 minutes from the Loop, is the strongest dispersion story in the country, and it costs almost nothing to cover from Chicago.
2. **Top three follow-on pilots:** (1) **Chicago suburbs + NW Indiana**; (2) **Minneapolis–St. Paul** (95% wholesale tax, the highest in the US, tied with Washington; untaxed Wisconsin across the river; Midwest smokeless culture); (3) **New York City** (a new **75% wholesale tax effective Sept 1, 2026** [V: NY Tax Department Notice N-26-2], so a fresh consumer price shock like Illinois's in 2025, plus extreme store density and lower-tax NJ/PA nearby). **St. Louis** is the strongest alternative #3: a metro split between Illinois (45%) and Missouri (no pouch tax), close to Chicago and cheap to test.
3. **College towns do not rank well on evidence.** Their adult (21+) populations are small, a large share of students are under 21 (a reputational and legal hazard for nicotine marketing), and most top college towns sit in low- or no-pouch-tax states (Madison, Columbus, Bloomington), so price dispersion is small. Madison is the best of them, because it is close to Chicago, not because it is a college town. **No college-town-first strategy.**
4. **No city-level ZYN usage data exists publicly.** National adult current pouch use is low and rising (≈0.4% current use in 2022 per JAMA 2024; past-30-day use 0.8% per JAMA Netw Open 2025; a young-adult panel shows 4.2% past-30-day in 2024) [S]. State data are sparse. Indiana reports **2% adult current use in 2024, about 5× its cited national figure** [V: Indiana Dept. of Health fact sheet, Dec 2025]. Smokeless-tobacco use is highest in the Midwest (3.2%) and lowest in the Northeast (1.2%) [V: CDC]. Demand scores below are therefore coarse proxies (adult population × regional smokeless culture), and **the ranking is most sensitive to demand assumptions**.

**Source conflicts recorded:** (a) The Tax Foundation's narrative says New York extended its tax "at the existing rate of 20 percent," while its own table says 75%. **The NY Tax Department notice confirms 75%.** (b) The Tax Foundation says Illinois "tripled the rate from 15 percent to 45 percent," while the Illinois Department of Revenue and the bill text say tobacco products went from 36% to 45% and the base was newly extended to pouches. The 15% figure is most plausibly the prior e-cigarette rate (register L11). (c) Maine: one source says 75% of wholesale, but the Tax Foundation says $3.54/oz. Use the Tax Foundation.

**Why taxes matter for this business [I]:** retail price dispersion within a market comes from markups (store type, location, competition) and from tax pass-through differences after a tax change. Across a border it comes mainly from tax differences. **High-tax states create both a higher price level (more absolute savings) and recent "repricing chaos"** (uneven pass-through right after a change), which is the product's best moment. Markets with new taxes in the last 12–18 months (Illinois 2025; Washington, Nebraska, Oregon, Maine in 2026; New York Sept 2026) are in that window now.

## 2. Scoring framework

| Code | Factor | Weight (base) | How scored (1–5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| D | Price dispersion potential | 25% | Pouch tax level, recency of a tax change, and a cross-border low-tax alternative within the metro or within ~30 min |
| P | Demand proxy | 20% | Metro adult population [M] × regional/state smokeless and pouch culture [V/S]; no city ZYN data exist |
| R | Retail density / coverage cost | 15% | Licensed tobacco retailers in a compact core; walkability for manual collection; sprawl lowers the score |
| S | Search and competitive gap | 10% | Absence of a store-level price competitor (Nicotinly's footprint is Southeast-focused: GA/FL/NC/SC) |
| O | Operating and regulatory ease | 15% | All-party-consent recording state, state autodialer or mini-TCPA restrictions, flavor bans that complicate listings (e.g., MA statewide flavored-tobacco ban), tobacco-policy climate |
| X | Proximity / pilot speed | 10% | Ability to run in-person sweeps from Chicago, and founder familiarity |
| C | Adult-student concentration | 5% | Large 21+ graduate and professional population; deliberately low weight, never a targeting criterion |

**Score = Σ (weight × sub-score)**, maximum 5.00.

| Distance ignored (X 0%) | **NYC (4.35)** · Chicago ext. (4.30) · Minneapolis (4.10) · St. Louis (3.65) · Portland–Vancouver (3.50) |
| College-weighted (C 20%) | Minneapolis (3.95) · Chicago ext. (3.95) · NYC (3.90) · St. Louis (3.50) · **Madison (3.30)** |

**Robustness:** the same three markets (Chicago extension, Minneapolis, NYC) fill the top three under every weighting. **NYC takes first place only if proximity is ignored**, e.g., if the founder can hire a local collector or moves there. **Houston and Dallas enter the top five only when demand dominates.** **Madison enters only when college concentration gets an implausibly high weight.**

**What would change the ranking (beyond weights):**
- Measured Chicago dispersion that is **mostly cross-border** (suburb vs. Indiana) rather than intra-city → favor in-metro-border markets: St. Louis, Portland–Vancouver.
- Evidence that **recency of a tax change** drives traffic (Search Console spikes around tax news) → favor NYC (Sept 2026), Washington (Jan 2026), Nebraska and Oregon (Jan 2026).
- A competitor appearing in a market → lower its S score.
- Counsel clearing automated calling only in Illinois → markets reachable by manual and community methods (dense cores: NYC, Minneapolis) keep their rank; sprawling Texas metros fall further.

## 6. Nearby expansion vs. a distant metro

| | Chicago suburbs + NW Indiana | Distant metro (Minneapolis or NYC) |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal cost | Lowest (founder can sweep in person; same Illinois legal regime; Indiana side manual-only) | Travel or local hire; a new state-law memo; new SEO footprint |
| Product value | **Extends the existing users' radius**: commuters and border shoppers; the "save $X by buying in Hammond" story | Proves the model generalizes beyond one tax regime |
| B2B value | Thickens one metro's panel (more valuable to a Chicago distributor) | Multi-metro coverage, which data buyers and acquirers need (Phase 3 §9 milestone: ≥3 metros) |
| Risk | Over-concentration in one market's tax story | Spreading thin before the model works anywhere |
| **Recommendation** | **Do first**, as part of the Chicago pilot | **Do second**, only after Chicago passes gates G1–G6; choose Minneapolis for an easy proof, or NYC for maximum upside |

## 7. Recommended follow-on pilots

1. **Chicago suburbs + NW Indiana border** (months 2–4 of the Chicago pilot). Collect manually in Indiana. Publish a "Chicago vs. Indiana pouch price gap" index, which is a newsworthy, linkable SEO asset.
2. **Minneapolis–St. Paul** (after the Chicago gates pass). Highest tax in the country, a Wisconsin border, Midwest demand, drivable from Chicago. Expected cost to useful coverage is roughly $1–1.5k manual plus the founder's time.
3. **New York City**, *or* **St. Louis** if the budget is tight. NYC has the largest upside and a fresh tax shock, but needs a local collector and an NY-specific legal check. St. Louis is a cheap test of the in-metro border thesis.

**Do not** prioritize college towns, Southern untaxed metros, or Massachusetts. Their dispersion and regulatory profiles are weaker, and youth-proximity risk is higher for college towns.
